Earthquake safety — the part that actually saves people

You are far more likely to be hurt by something falling on you than by a building coming down. That is true in almost every earthquake that is not catastrophic, and it is the reason the cheapest preparations are also the most effective ones.

When the shaking starts

Drop. Cover. Hold on.

  • Drop to your hands and knees before the shaking drops you.
  • Cover your head and neck. Get under a sturdy table if one is close. If not, get next to an interior wall away from windows and anything that can fall.
  • Hold on to your shelter and move with it until the shaking stops.

Two pieces of advice you have probably heard that are wrong:

Do not run outside. Nevada's own geological survey says this plainly. Most injuries happen to people moving during the shaking, and the outside of a building — glass, parapets, roof tiles, power lines — is the worst place to stand.

Do not stand in a doorway. This was reasonable advice for old adobe houses where the door frame was the strongest part of the structure. In any modern building the doorway is no stronger than the rest of the wall, it does not protect your head, and the door itself can swing and injure you.

Before it happens: secure what will fall on you

This is an afternoon of work and it is the highest-value thing on this page.

  • Strap the water heater to the wall studs. An unstrapped heater walks, snaps its gas and water lines, and gives you a fire and a flood at the same moment — while destroying the 40+ gallons of drinkable water you were about to badly need.
  • Anchor tall furniture — bookcases, wardrobes, shelving, filing cabinets — into studs, not drywall.
  • Get heavy things off high shelves, and get them away from beds and from where people sit.
  • Secure mirrors, TVs, and hanging objects, especially over beds and couches.
  • Know where the gas shutoff is and keep a wrench with it. Shut it off only if you smell gas or hear it leaking — once it is off, the utility has to come relight it, and after a large earthquake that may take weeks.

Longer-term fixes to the building itself

  • Bolt the house to its foundation if it is not already.
  • Brace cripple walls — the short stud walls between the foundation and the floor in a raised-floor house. Unbraced, they fold over and the house slides off.
  • If you are in unreinforced masonry, understand that this is the single most dangerous common building type in an earthquake, and that retrofits exist.

Water — the one you will actually run out of

If you do only one thing on this page, do this one. Keep water in the garage. It is the supply that runs out first, the one you cannot improvise, and the one that matters in every disaster, not just earthquakes — a broken main, a power failure, a heat emergency, a contamination notice.

  • One gallon per person per day is the floor, not the goal. That is the standard minimum for drinking and basic sanitation. Three days is the usual advice; two weeks is the target worth actually hitting.
  • In the Las Vegas valley, plan on more than the standard number. This is the Mojave Desert. A summer emergency here happens at 105 °F or worse, quite possibly with no air conditioning, and a body in that heat needs considerably more water than the minimum figure assumes. So do children, anyone ill, anyone pregnant or nursing, and anyone doing the physical work of digging out and cleaning up.
  • Do not forget pets. They need their own supply and they are routinely left out of the calculation.
  • The garage is the right place for it. It is the one part of the house with room for a real quantity, it keeps the water out of the way, and it is easy to rotate. Date the containers and replace them on a schedule you will actually remember — a birthday, a time change, the ShakeOut drill in October.
  • Your water heater holds forty-plus gallons of drinking water — but only if it is still standing. That is the second reason to strap it: an unstrapped heater does not just start a fire, it drains your largest emergency reserve into the floor.

There is a reason local engineers push this harder than the national guidance does. The Las Vegas valley imports nearly all of its water and pumps it uphill from Lake Mead to get it here. That is a long chain of pipelines, pump stations, and electrical power, and shaking can break any link in it. Water is the thing this valley is least able to improvise.

The rest of the kit

  • Food that needs no cooking, and a way to open it.
  • A flashlight and spare batteries — not candles, which are how post-earthquake fires start in a house that may be leaking gas.
  • A first aid kit, and any medication you cannot skip.
  • Sturdy shoes and work gloves kept under the bed. Broken glass is the injury that gets almost everyone, on the way out of a dark room.
  • A power bank, a battery or crank radio, cash in small bills, and a blanket.

Have a plan before you need one

  • Pick a meeting place outside the house, and a second one outside the neighbourhood.
  • Choose an out-of-state contact everyone checks in with. Local phone networks jam immediately; long distance and text messages often still get through when calls will not.
  • Expect aftershocks. They can be large enough to bring down what the first shock weakened, so get out of a damaged building and stay out.

Why this matters here, of all places

Nevada is the third most seismically active state in the country, behind only Alaska and California. Since the 1850s the state has had 63 earthquakes of magnitude 5.5 or greater. On average Nevada gets a magnitude 6 about every ten years and a magnitude 7 about every twenty-seven. The whole state sits in the Basin and Range — crust that is actively pulling apart, which is why it is covered in faults — and the Walker Lane, running roughly 600 miles along the Nevada–California border, is the most active part of it.

Almost nobody who lives here knows any of that. The intervals are long enough that a person can live a whole life in Nevada, never feel a serious earthquake, and reasonably conclude there is no hazard. The faults do not work on a human attention span.

And the Las Vegas valley adds the problem this page started with: it is a deep basin full of soft sediment sitting on rock. Soft ground shakes harder than rock does, and it shakes hardest at the slow, long periods that tall buildings are most vulnerable to. Move the sliders above and you can watch that happen.
